IN-PROCESS LANGUAGE PRIMITIVES — the eight designs, compared
=============================================================

CATEGORY: prior art for the Task/Job/Future abstraction (in-process group)
ADDED: 2026-08-19
STATUS: the least important group for this project, and the best documented.
  Read the table, read section 3 (progress) and section 5 (the verdict), skip
  the rest unless designing the per-language binding.
WHY IT IS THE LEAST IMPORTANT: not one of these survives process exit. Not one
  can be observed by another process. Six of the eight have no progress concept
  at all. They are the BINDING layer, not the abstraction. Their value here is
  (a) they tell us which idioms each language's users expect a job handle to be
  projected into, and (b) two of them — C#'s CancellationToken and Kotlin's
  structured Job tree — contain ideas worth stealing outright.

2. PER-LANGUAGE NOTES — only what is load-bearing
---------------------------------------------------------------------------

C# — Task + CancellationToken. Does "most complete design" hold up?
  Partly, and the part that holds up is not Task.
  - Lifecycle: TaskStatus has eight values (Created, WaitingForActivation,
    WaitingToRun, Running, WaitingForChildrenToComplete, RanToCompletion,
    Canceled, Faulted). Richer than anyone else's. Terminal states distinguish
    cancelled from faulted, which most designs conflate.
  - The genuinely great idea is CANCELLATION AS A SEPARATE, PASSABLE,
    COMPOSABLE VALUE: CancellationTokenSource owns the trigger, CancellationToken
    is a read-only capability you hand down, CreateLinkedTokenSource composes,
    Register attaches cleanup, and cancellation is a first-class *argument* to
    every async API rather than a method on the handle. Splitting "who may
    cancel" from "who observes cancellation" is a capability-security decision
    and it is the single best idea in Group A. Go copied it (context), Rust
    copied it (tokio_util CancellationToken), Java did not, JS eventually did
    (AbortController/AbortSignal is the same split under different names).
  - What does NOT hold up:
      * Cancellation is cooperative only. .NET Core removed Thread.Abort; there
        is no forced kill. Correct choice, but it means "cancelled" is a request.
      * A CancellationToken is a pointer into this process's heap. It cannot be
        serialised, transferred, or observed from outside. The best-designed
        cancellation primitive in Group A is exactly as useless across a process
        boundary as the worst.
      * Progress is out-of-band (IProgress<T>), the caller supplies the sink,
        and a caller who is not there (crashed, or never was the submitter) gets
        nothing.
      * Task conflates "the operation" with "the future value", and is eager,
        hot, and already running when you receive it. There is no "submitted but
        not started" that you can inspect.
  - THE COUNTEREXAMPLE WORTH MORE THAN Task ITSELF: WinRT's async model.
    Four ABI interfaces — IAsyncAction, IAsyncActionWithProgress<TProgress>,
    IAsyncOperation<TResult>, IAsyncOperationWithProgress<TResult,TProgress> —
    all deriving from IAsyncInfo, whose members are Id(), Status(), ErrorCode(),
    Cancel(), Close(). (Source: learn.microsoft.com IAsyncOperationWithProgress
    and C++/WinRT concurrency docs, fetched 2026-08-19.)
    This matters for three reasons: it is an ABI, not a language feature, so it
    is genuinely cross-language (C++, C#, JS, Rust via windows-rs bind the same
    interfaces); PROGRESS IS IN THE TYPE, not bolted on; and there is an `Id`
    on the handle. It is the closest thing to a cross-language async ABI anyone
    has shipped. Its limits are equally instructive: Id is process-local, the
    handle is a COM pointer, and Close() is a lifetime verb, not a job verb.
    So even the one cross-language async ABI in existence did not cross a
    process boundary. Worth studying before claiming the design space is empty.

Kotlin — Job, Deferred, structured concurrency
  - Job has an explicit, documented state machine: New -> Active -> Completing ->
    Completed, with Cancelling -> Cancelled as the failure path, exposed as three
    booleans (isActive, isCompleted, isCancelled) rather than an enum.
  - THE IDEA TO STEAL IS THE TREE. Every coroutine has a parent Job; a scope does
    not complete until its children do; cancelling a parent cancels all children;
    an uncaught failure in a child cancels the parent (unless a SupervisorJob
    breaks that edge). Cancellation propagates DOWN, failure propagates UP.
    Nobody else in Group A makes the parent/child edge a first-class,
    inspectable, overridable thing (Job.children is enumerable).
    Direct relevance: our chain is app -> system service -> NAS. That IS a job
    tree spanning three processes, and Kotlin is the design that treats the tree
    as the object rather than each node in isolation. What a durable version
    needs that Kotlin cannot give is a parent id that is meaningful in another
    process — which is just to say: put a `parent` field in the record.
  - Cancellation is cooperative and checked at suspension points; a tight
    non-suspending loop is uncancellable unless it calls ensureActive()/yield().
    CancellationException is special-cased so it does not look like a failure.
  - Composition: awaitAll, select. No progress concept; the idiomatic answer is
    to emit a Flow alongside, i.e. a second channel, i.e. the same out-of-band
    answer as C#.

Java — CompletableFuture, and Loom / StructuredTaskScope
  - CompletableFuture is the cautionary tale. Its javadoc states that
    mayInterruptIfRunning "has no effect in this implementation because
    interrupts are not used to control processing" (verified against Oracle's
    Java SE javadoc, 2026-08-19). So cancel() marks the future cancelled and
    LEAVES THE WORK RUNNING. Every downstream stage sees CancellationException
    while the thread keeps burning CPU and network.
    The reason is structural and it is the most useful lesson in Group A: a
    CompletableFuture is not bound to any executing thread — `new
    CompletableFuture<>()` is legal and has no work behind it — so the handle
    genuinely does not know what to cancel. THE HANDLE WAS SEPARATED FROM THE
    WORK AND CANCELLATION FELL THROUGH THE GAP. That is precisely the topology of
    our problem (the handle is here, the work is in another process), so this is
    the failure mode we must design against, not an aside. The fix is that a
    cancel request must be ROUTED TO THE EXECUTOR by identity, not invoked on a
    local object — which is exactly what LRO's CancelOperation(name) does.
  - Composition: allOf/anyOf, thenCombine, thenCompose. Rich and much copied.
  - StructuredTaskScope (Project Loom) is Java adopting the Kotlin/Swift model.
    Still a preview at JDK 25 — JEP 505 "Structured Concurrency (Fifth Preview)"
    — with a further JEP 525 sixth preview after it. JDK 25 reworked the API:
    obtained via a static open() rather than a constructor, fork(Callable) runs
    the subtask on a virtual thread, join() throws subtask exceptions, and
    failure policy moved into pluggable Joiners. Six previews is itself data:
    the committee has changed its mind about this API repeatedly, which is a
    reason to be suspicious of anyone (including us) who thinks the ergonomics of
    task scoping are obvious.
  - No progress, in either API.

Rust — Future, tokio::JoinHandle, cancellation by drop
  - A Future is inert until polled, and the runtime owns polling. This is the
    only language here where the handle-to-work relationship is inverted:
    dropping the future IS cancellation. No cancel method needed; no cooperation
    needed; the work simply stops being polled at its last await point.
  - Consequences that matter to us:
      * Cancellation is instant, guaranteed, and unobservable by the work.
        There is no `async Drop`, so a future cancelled mid-flight cannot run
        async cleanup. "Cancellation safety" is a documented property authors
        must reason about per-function (tokio's select! docs are largely about
        this). A design where cancellation cannot be caught is the opposite
        extreme from Java's, and it is also wrong for us: an out-of-process job
        MUST be able to run cleanup (delete the partial file, release the lock).
      * tokio::spawn returns a JoinHandle, which detaches: dropping the
        JoinHandle does NOT cancel the task, unlike dropping the future. You
        must call abort() (or AbortHandle::abort), which cancels at the next
        await point. JoinError distinguishes is_cancelled() from is_panic().
        This is the closest Group A comes to "the handle and the work have
        independent lifetimes", and note it required a distinct type to express.
      * tokio_util::sync::CancellationToken re-imports the C# design because
        drop-cancellation is not enough once you have spawned tasks.
  - Composition: futures::join!/try_join!, tokio::select!, JoinSet. No progress.

Go — and there is no future type, which is the point
  - Go has no Future, Promise, Task, or Deferred in its standard library, by
    design. The idioms are: a goroutine plus a channel; sync.WaitGroup;
    golang.org/x/sync/errgroup (Go, Wait, and since v0.6-ish SetLimit and
    TryGo), which returns the FIRST non-nil error and cancels a derived context.
  - context.Context is the cancellation and deadline carrier: Done() <-chan
    struct{}, Err(), Deadline(), Value(). Extensions: WithCancelCause + Cause
    (Go 1.20), AfterFunc + WithoutCancel + WithDeadlineCause/WithTimeoutCause
    (Go 1.21). (Source: go.dev/doc/go1.20 and go1.21 release notes.)
    context is the C# CancellationToken idea with a value bag stapled on.
  - THIS IS DIRECTLY CONSEQUENTIAL because this project is written in Go. There
    is no ambient expectation to satisfy, no `Task<T>` shape users will demand
    the facade conform to, and no temptation to make the handle a live object
    with methods that secretly close over runtime state. Go pushes you toward
    exactly the right answer: a job id value, a store, functions that take a
    context. Go's *absence* of a future type is an advantage here, and any Go
    API that returns a `<-chan Result` for a job that lives in another process
    would be an error — a channel cannot be persisted, enumerated, or reopened
    after a crash.
  - The one thing Go lacks that we must supply: context cancellation stops at
    the process boundary. `ctx.Done()` firing must be translated into a
    Cancel(jobID) RPC by the binding; it cannot propagate by itself.
  - No progress. Also no standard way to say "this operation is 40% done"
    anywhere in the Go standard library.

JavaScript — Promise + AbortController
  - Promise: eager, single-shot, no cancellation whatsoever, no progress. The
    original Promises/A+ design deliberately omitted both; a proposal for
    cancellable promises was withdrawn in 2016.
  - AbortController/AbortSignal (WHATWG DOM, also in Node) is a SEPARATE object
    passed as an argument — the C# split again: controller.abort(reason) is the
    trigger, signal is the read-only observer, signal.addEventListener('abort'),
    signal.throwIfAborted(). AbortSignal.timeout() and AbortSignal.any() compose
    them. The convention is that cancellation rejects the promise with an
    AbortError DOMException.
  - Composition: Promise.all / allSettled / race / any. `allSettled` is the one
    worth noting — it is the only widely used combinator that reports per-child
    outcome rather than collapsing to first-failure, which is what a job tree
    with partial failures actually needs (compare LRO metadata's "partial
    failures", and K8s Job's succeeded/failed counts).
  - Progress: none. The old XMLHttpRequest had onprogress; fetch removed it and
    the replacement is to read the response body as a ReadableStream and count
    bytes yourself — i.e. the web platform CONSCIOUSLY MOVED AWAY from a progress
    callback toward "you count what passes through you". That is the same
    conclusion research/transfer/SUMMARY.txt reached about SMB ("you count your
    own bytes, which is the correct answer").

Python — asyncio.Task / Future, and concurrent.futures
  - asyncio.Task.cancel() throws CancelledError into the coroutine at its next
    await; the coroutine may catch it (and, badly, swallow it). Python 3.11
    added cancelling()/uncancel() precisely to let nested cancellation scopes
    tell "someone above me cancelled" from "I cancelled my own child" — a real
    problem that appears the moment cancellation composes. 3.11 also added
    TaskGroup (structured concurrency) and asyncio.timeout.
  - concurrent.futures.Future is the important one for us because it is the only
    Group A primitive that spans processes at all (ProcessPoolExecutor). And it
    is a demonstration of the limits: Future.cancel() returns False and does
    nothing if the work has already started, because there is no mechanism to
    interrupt a worker process; only queued items can be cancelled. A handle to
    work in another process, with no cancellation and no progress — this is what
    you get if you take the in-process design and naively stretch it across a
    process boundary. It is the naive answer, it exists, and it is inadequate.
  - Exceptions are re-raised on result(). Progress: none.

Swift — structured concurrency
  - Task, async let, TaskGroup / ThrowingTaskGroup, actors. Cancellation is
    cooperative and inherited by the task tree: Task.isCancelled,
    Task.checkCancellation() (throws CancellationError), and
    withTaskCancellationHandler for immediate reaction. A cancelled Swift task
    keeps running until it checks — same model as Kotlin.
  - Notable: cancellation is a FLAG ON THE TASK, and the tree propagates it
    automatically to child tasks; a detached Task deliberately opts out of the
    tree. Same shape as Kotlin's Job/SupervisorJob.
  - No progress in Swift Concurrency. Foundation's Progress (NSProgress) is the
    separate answer, and it is the most interesting one in Group A for our
    purposes: Progress supports implicit parent/child composition
    (totalUnitCount / pendingUnitCount), cancellation and pause handlers, AND —
    on macOS — CROSS-PROCESS OBSERVATION via publish()/
    addSubscriber(forFileURL:withPublishingHandler:), plus NSXPCConnection
    integration for progress across an XPC boundary. (Sources: Apple Foundation
    docs; oleb.net "NSProgress", 2014-03; Foundation release notes for macOS
    10.13 note NSXPCConnection support for discrete NSProgress. Fetched
    2026-08-19.)
    So: exactly one mainstream platform shipped a cross-process progress
    protocol, in 2014, keyed by file URL, and it is Apple-only and largely
    unused outside Finder-style copy UIs. That is both an existence proof and a
    warning about adoption.

3. PROGRESS: THE HOLE IN ALL OF THEM
---------------------------------------------------------------------------
  Stated plainly, because the brief asks for it: SIX OF THE EIGHT HAVE NO
  PROGRESS CONCEPT AT ALL — Kotlin, Java (both APIs), Rust, Go, JavaScript,
  Python. Not "weak"; absent.

  The two that have something have it OUTSIDE the task type:
    - C#: IProgress<T> / Progress<T>, a caller-supplied callback sink, marshalled
      to the captured SynchronizationContext. Bound to the caller's lifetime.
    - Swift/Obj-C: Foundation Progress, a separate tree of objects, with the
      cross-process publish/subscribe mechanism noted above.
  And one ABI has it in the type: WinRT's *WithProgress interfaces.

  Why they all omit it, and this is worth taking seriously rather than treating
  as an oversight: progress is not part of the *contract* of a computation. It is
  telemetry. It is not monotonic in general, its unit is domain-specific, its
  cost is nonzero, and a function that returns a value has no obligation to
  narrate. For an in-process future, adding it would tax every user for the
  benefit of a few.

  The reason it is unavoidable for US is that the caller cannot see the work at
  all. In-process, if you want to know what a computation is doing you can
  instrument it, profile it, or read its code. Across a process boundary with a
  swappable implementation, the ONLY thing the caller can ever know is what the
  record says. Progress is not a convenience here; it is the entire observability
  surface, and METHOD §2 makes that the reason the boundary exists at all.

  Design consequence, from the evidence: standardise a MINIMAL, EXPLICITLY
  BEST-EFFORT progress triple in the record, and put everything richer in the
  opaque blob. The failures to avoid are visible in the survey — WorkManager
  discards progress at terminal state (so it cannot be used for audit),
  C#/IProgress ties it to a live caller (so a crash loses it), and LRO refuses to
  standardise any field (so no generic client can render it).

4. CANCELLATION: COOPERATIVE OR FORCED?
---------------------------------------------------------------------------
  COOPERATIVE, EVERYWHERE, WITHOUT EXCEPTION — C#, Kotlin, Java (both), Go,
  JS, Python asyncio, Swift. The two apparent exceptions prove the rule:
    - Rust drop-cancellation is not forced cancellation of running code; it is
      the runtime declining to poll again. It cannot interrupt a blocking
      section either, and it forfeits async cleanup.
    - Java's CompletableFuture.cancel is not cancellation at all; it abandons
      the handle and leaves the work running.
  Forced cancellation was tried and withdrawn: Thread.stop deprecated in Java
  1.2, Thread.Abort removed in .NET Core. The industry has a settled answer and
  it is "you may ask; you may not compel".

  Two structural variants exist and the difference is real:
    - CANCEL THE HANDLE (Java CF, tokio JoinHandle::abort) — the handle is the
      addressee.
    - CANCEL VIA A SEPARATE CAPABILITY (C# CancellationToken, Go context,
      JS AbortSignal, tokio_util CancellationToken) — the trigger is a distinct
      object that can be shared, linked, and handed to code that must not be able
      to cancel. Three independent language communities converged on this after
      shipping the first variant. That convergence is worth respecting.

  For an out-of-process job the second variant degenerates: there is nothing to
  hand down, because the work is not in your address space. What replaces it is
  cancellation-by-identity — Cancel(jobID) routed to whoever is executing —
  plus, on the executor side, a local CancellationToken/context that the
  executor derives from the incoming request. So the interface has ONE cancel
  verb taking an id, and each binding wires it to its native mechanism. And per
  LRO and every distributed system in Group B: it is a best-effort REQUEST whose
  outcome is observed by re-reading the record, never a synchronous guarantee.

5. VERDICT — WHAT GROUP A CONTRIBUTES
---------------------------------------------------------------------------
  Best in-process design overall: C#'s, but for the CancellationToken split
  rather than for Task. The claim that Task is "the most complete design" holds
  only within the in-process frame; Task's completeness is in its state enum and
  its combinators, and both are the least transferable parts. Kotlin's Job tree
  is a better *model* and Rust's drop-cancellation is a better *mechanism* for
  the problems each solves.

  Three things to steal from Group A, and only three:
    1. C#/Go/JS: separate the cancellation TRIGGER from the cancellation
       OBSERVER. Across processes this becomes: cancel by id at the interface,
       native token inside each implementation.
    2. Kotlin/Swift: the parent/child job tree with cancel-down / fail-up, and
       an explicit escape hatch (SupervisorJob, detached Task) for the edges you
       do not want to propagate. Our record needs a `parent` field and a stated
       propagation rule; the app -> service -> NAS chain is a tree already.
    3. C# TaskStatus and Rust's JoinError: keep CANCELLED distinct from FAILED
       as terminal states. LRO does not (cancellation surfaces as an error) and
       that loses information every operator wants at 3am.

  One thing to steal from adjacent-to-Group-A prior art, and it is the most
  valuable item on this page: WinRT's *WithProgress interfaces plus IAsyncInfo's
  `Id` — the only shipped cross-language async ABI that put progress in the type
  and an identity on the handle. Study it before designing ours.

  And the thing NOT to do: do not model the interface on `Future<T>`. Section 2's
  Python concurrent.futures entry is what that produces when stretched across a
  process boundary — a handle that cannot cancel started work, cannot report
  progress, cannot be re-obtained after a crash, and cannot be enumerated. The
  detailed indictment is in SUMMARY.txt.
